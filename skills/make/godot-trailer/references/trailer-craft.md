# Trailer craft — the rules, and why

A game trailer is not a summary of the game and not a short film. It is an argument
made to a stranger who owes you nothing, who decides within seconds whether to keep
watching and leaves the moment they are bored or confused. Almost every rule below
follows from that. These are practical guidelines, not a universal formula: a
cinematic announcement, a store-page trailer and a social clip have different jobs,
and most weak trailers come from one video trying to do all of them.

## The three questions

In roughly this order, the viewer asks: **What is this? What would I actually do in
it? Why should I care, when I already have other games?** A good trailer answers all
three quickly, mostly with footage. A bad one answers them late, out of order, or
not at all, usually because it was built to impress rather than to inform.

## Which trailer?

Decide first. The default for `godot-trailer` is the **store-page gameplay trailer**.

| Type | Job | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| `store` | convert strangers sitting next to the button | every rule applies strictest; 30–90 s; gameplay first |
| `announcement` | intrigue and memorability | more room for tone, mystery, cinematic framing; still needs a glimpse of what the player does, or intrigue never becomes interest in the *game* |
| `launch` | audience already exists | may show more of the game, add real social proof (cited quotes, awards), lead with the release date |
| vertical / social | stop the scroll | its own native 9:16 cut; hook in the first second or two; one moment; title last. A separate request, never a crop of the wide trailer |

## The rules

1. **Choose one audience and one goal.** Wishlist, play a demo, buy, download. Choose
   footage that answers that audience's questions: a strategy player needs meaningful
   decisions on screen; a horror player needs vulnerability and tension.
2. **Gameplay in the first five seconds.** The opening is the most-watched part of the
   trailer, so don't spend it on studio or publisher logos, a fade from black, or a
   slow atmospheric pan that could belong to any game. Logos go at the end. The one
   exception is a studio or franchise the audience already cares about, whose logo *is*
   the hook; an unknown developer doesn't have that.
3. **Lead with the hook, and establish the identity early.** Open on the single most
   distinctive thing: the mechanic, image or moment nobody else has. Viewers need
   familiarity (the category: "a platformer") and a reason to care (the difference:
   "you reverse gravity to turn hazards into pathways"). Name the genre with footage
   and reach the hook within roughly the first 10–15 seconds. If the hook can't be
   said in a sentence, the trailer can't find it, and no editing will invent it. That
   is a design problem showing, worth learning while the game can still change.
4. **Show the verb: what the player actually does.** People buy what they'll do, not
   what they'll see. By the end a viewer should be able to say what they press and
   what happens when they press it. Keep the player character on screen, early, doing
   things, and build at least one clear **situation → action → consequence** sequence.
   Environment flythroughs, lore narration and cutscenes don't answer "what do I do?"
5. **One idea, escalated; not a feature list.** Pick the core idea and spend the whole
   runtime making it bigger, stranger or more intense. A first shelter grows into an
   expressive settlement; a simple puzzle rule meets a surprising combination. Cut
   everything that doesn't serve the idea, including features you're proud of. A
   feature tour feels thorough to the developer and flat to the viewer, and gives each
   mechanic three seconds so none lands.
6. **Structure: hook, establish, escalate, peak, title, button.** A compressed three
   acts. After the peak comes the title card, then optionally a **button**, a short
   beat (a joke, a surprise, one last great moment) that rewards the people who stayed.
   Vary the rhythm as you build: a brief drop or pause just before the peak makes the
   peak land.
7. **Legibility beats intensity.** Fast cuts of action the viewer can't follow read as
   noise. Every shot must be readable in the time it's on screen: who the player is,
   what the threat is, what just happened. Give viewers time to locate the player,
   recognise the action and see its result. Establish the game *before* you speed up:
   cut rate should follow how well the viewer understands the game by that point, not
   how exciting you want it to feel. Keep interface elements that explain a mechanic
   (health, combo meter, resource counts); hide only clutter. Check every frame for
   visual bugs.
8. **Match the pacing to the experience.** Cozy games can breathe; horror can linger;
   action can accelerate. Change the information and the emotional intensity as it
   progresses. Constant fast cutting is not automatically engaging. Move on when an
   idea has landed, and use contrast between sections.
9. **Keep it short.** For most store-page trailers, 30–90 seconds; 60–90 is a good
   first target. A long trailer usually means nobody made choices and kept material
   because cutting it hurt. If a beat isn't earning its seconds, it's costing viewers.
10. **Make it work with the sound off, then make the sound great.** Feeds and many
    storefronts start muted, so the images alone must carry the premise and the key
    information. Then, for viewers with sound: edit to the music, let real game audio
    punch through (impacts, abilities, the game's signature sounds), and balance so the
    important sound is clear at each moment, with room for pauses and impact. Music
    that doesn't match the game's tone ("epic" stock over a cozy game, or the reverse)
    is one of the most damaging choices: it advertises a different game.
11. **Text is a tax.** Every card stops momentum because the viewer stops watching to
    read. Use text only for what footage can't show (player count, platform, a one-line
    premise), in a few words, and never adjectives. "Epic", "immersive",
    "breathtaking" are claims; footage is evidence.
12. **Make an accurate promise.** Capture from the real game and keep the promise.
    A misleading trailer buys short-term wishlists at the cost of long-term refunds
    and bad reviews. Treat every edit as a claim: does it imply a mechanic, a level of
    freedom or a visual quality the game doesn't deliver? Clearly separate cinematics
    or concept footage from gameplay if they could be mistaken. Good capture craft is
    fine: play skilfully, rehearse the run, use camera tools to get clean shots, hide
    HUD that only adds clutter. Dishonest capture is not: cheats, edited-in features,
    states the player can't reach.
13. **Finish with one next step.** The last card shows a readable title, the platform,
    the release date or "coming soon" (or what's honestly true), and a single action:
    "Wishlist on Steam", "Play the demo". Hold it long enough to read. One action, not
    four social handles. Never fade to black.

## A 60-second sketch (guidance, not a formula)

| About | Purpose | Show |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 s | attract attention | a distinctive, immediately readable moment |
| 5–15 s | establish the premise | the player's role, the core action, the main hook |
| 15–35 s | demonstrate the appeal | connected gameplay showing choices and consequences |
| 35–50 s | expand the possibilities | complications, variety, progression, higher stakes |
| ~50 s | deliver the payoff | a memorable reveal, achievement, joke or suspenseful moment |
| 55–60 s | prompt action | title, availability, one call to action |

## Failure modes

- **The logo opening.** Spends the most valuable seconds on the least interesting content.
- **The lore crawl.** Narrates a world the viewer has no reason to care about yet.
- **The flythrough.** Beautiful environments, no player. Answers "what is this?" and
  skips "what do I do?"
- **The feature tour.** Three seconds per mechanic; none lands.
- **Tone mismatch.** Grim orchestral over a cozy game, or the reverse.
- **Chaos as excitement.** Cuts faster than the viewer can follow: busy, not thrilling.
- **The adjective stack.** Cards that make claims the footage never backs up.
- **The misleading shot.** A mechanic, freedom or fidelity the game doesn't have.

## The falsifiable test

Don't judge the trailer by how it feels to you; you already know the game. Show it to
someone with no context.

1. Stop it at 10 seconds. Ask what kind of game it is and what the player does.
2. Let it finish. Ask what's different about this game, which moment interested them
   most, and where they were confused or bored.
3. Watch their face, not the screen. Where attention drifts is where to cut.
4. Show a *different* person the trailer **with the sound off** and repeat the questions.
5. Ask what they would do next.

If they can't answer the three core questions, the trailer hasn't done its job, however
good it looks. **Revise the footage and sequence before adding more polish.**
